Co-Parenting & Parenting Plan Mediation — Building a Framework for Your Family’s Future

Divorce or separation changes the relationship between partners, but it doesn’t end the relationship between parents. Co-parenting mediation helps you create a clear, comprehensive parenting plan that reduces conflict, protects your children, and gives both parents a roadmap for raising their kids cooperatively — even when cooperation feels impossible.

At Compass Mediation Group, lead mediator Alisa Kharis works with parents to develop parenting agreements that are detailed, realistic, and built to last. With a Juris Doctorate from Seattle University School of Law, 16 years of courtroom experience as a former practicing attorney who handled family law cases in Washington state courts, and a Master Mediator certification from the International Mediation Association (IMA) — plus over 25 years of mediation experience and education — Alisa helps parents move past the emotions of divorce and focus on the practical decisions their children need them to make.

All sessions are virtual, so you can participate from anywhere in the country.

What Is Co-Parenting Mediation?

Co-parenting mediation is a facilitated negotiation process where separated or divorced parents work with a neutral mediator to resolve parenting disputes and create or revise a parenting plan.

Unlike court proceedings — where a judge imposes a schedule based on limited information — mediation gives you the ability to design an arrangement that reflects your family’s unique needs, your children’s personalities, and each parent’s real-world schedule.

Co-parenting mediation is used for:

  • Creating an initial parenting plan during divorce or separation
  • Modifying an existing plan when circumstances change
  • Resolving ongoing co-parenting conflicts before they escalate to court
  • Addressing specific issues like holiday scheduling, relocation, or decision-making disagreements

What a Parenting Plan Includes

A well-drafted parenting plan is the foundation of successful co-parenting. It eliminates ambiguity and gives both parents clear expectations. Here’s what a comprehensive parenting plan should address:

📅

Residential Schedules

The day-to-day and week-to-week schedule that determines where your children live. Options include:

  • Alternating weeks (one week with each parent)
  • A 2-2-3 rotation (two days with one parent, two with the other, then three with the first)
  • A 3-4-4-3 schedule
  • Primary residence with one parent and scheduled time with the other
  • Custom arrangements that fit your family’s needs

There is no single “right” schedule. The best arrangement depends on your children’s ages, your work commitments, the distance between homes, and your children’s school and activity obligations.

🎄

Holiday and Special Occasion Schedules

Holidays are one of the most emotionally charged scheduling issues. A good parenting plan specifies:

  • How major holidays are divided (Thanksgiving, Christmas/Hanukkah, Easter, Fourth of July, etc.)
  • Whether holidays alternate yearly or are split within the day
  • Arrangements for birthdays, Mother’s Day, Father’s Day, and other meaningful dates
  • How school breaks (winter, spring, summer) are divided
🧳

Vacation and Travel

  • How much uninterrupted vacation time each parent is entitled to
  • Notice requirements before booking travel
  • Rules about out-of-state or international travel
  • Passport and travel document arrangements
💼

Decision-Making Authority

Major decisions about your children’s lives need a clear process. Your parenting plan should specify how you’ll handle:

  • Education — school choice, tutoring, special education services
  • Healthcare — medical treatments, therapy, dental care, vaccinations
  • Religious upbringing — if the parents have different beliefs or practices
  • Extracurricular activities — sports, camps, lessons, and the associated costs

You can agree to make these decisions jointly, assign certain categories to each parent, or establish a tie-breaking mechanism if you can’t agree.

📱

Communication Protocols

Clear communication rules prevent misunderstandings and reduce conflict. Your plan can include:

  • Preferred methods of communication between parents (email, text, co-parenting app)
  • Response time expectations for non-emergency communications
  • Rules about discussing the other parent in front of the children
  • How and when children can communicate with the non-residential parent (phone calls, FaceTime, etc.)
  • Guidelines for sharing information about school events, medical appointments, and activities
💵

Financial Arrangements Beyond Child Support

While child support is typically addressed separately, a parenting plan can also cover:

  • How extracurricular activity costs are shared
  • Who provides health insurance and how uncovered medical expenses are divided
  • Clothing, school supplies, and personal items for the child
  • How college savings or future educational expenses will be handled
🕊

Dispute Resolution

Even the best parenting plan can’t anticipate every situation. Including a dispute resolution clause — such as agreeing to return to mediation before filing a court motion — can save both parents time, money, and stress in the future.

Modifying an Existing Parenting Plan

Parenting plans that worked when your children were toddlers may not make sense when they’re teenagers. Life changes — job relocations, new relationships, evolving needs of growing children — often require adjustments to existing agreements.

Mediation is the most efficient way to modify a parenting plan. Rather than hiring attorneys and filing a court motion, you and your co-parent can sit down with a mediator and negotiate updated terms. Common triggers for modification include:

  • A significant change in either parent’s work schedule or location
  • A child’s changing developmental, social, or academic needs
  • One parent’s desire to relocate
  • The introduction of a new partner or stepparent into the household
  • A child reaching an age where their preferences should be considered
  • The original plan simply isn’t working as intended

Modified agreements reached in mediation can be submitted to the court to replace the original order, giving them full legal enforceability.

Co-Parenting After Divorce — When It Feels Impossible

Let’s be honest: co-parenting with someone you’ve chosen to separate from is hard. Co-parenting with someone who was controlling, dismissive, or uncooperative during the marriage can feel nearly impossible.

Mediation helps in these situations because it provides structure. You’re not trying to “get along” — you’re building a system. A detailed parenting plan with clear rules means you don’t need to have a good relationship with your co-parent. You need a good plan.

Co-Parenting with a Difficult Ex

If your co-parent is high-conflict, manipulative, or simply uncooperative, mediation offers several advantages over court:

  • A neutral third party controls the conversation. Alisa ensures both parents are heard and that the conversation stays productive. With 16 years of courtroom experience handling contentious cases — including criminal law, family law, and civil litigation across multiple Washington state counties — she has managed far more difficult dynamics than most mediators have encountered. There’s no room for bullying or steamrolling.
  • Communication is structured. Your parenting plan can include specific communication protocols — like requiring all non-emergency communication to go through a co-parenting app — that minimize direct conflict.
  • Boundaries are built into the plan. A comprehensive parenting plan leaves fewer gray areas for a difficult co-parent to exploit.
  • You maintain control. In court, a judge decides. In mediation, you have a voice in every decision.

Why Virtual Co-Parenting Mediation Works

All Compass Mediation Group sessions are conducted virtually, and for co-parenting mediation, this format offers unique benefits:

  • No shared waiting room. You don’t have to sit next to your ex in a lobby. You log in from your own space, on your own terms.
  • Reduced emotional intensity. The physical distance of a virtual session can help both parents stay calmer and more focused.
  • Flexible scheduling. Virtual sessions are easier to fit around work and childcare obligations — no commute, no taking time off.
  • Nationwide access. Whether you and your co-parent live in the same city or across the country, you can both participate from anywhere.
  • Easy document sharing. Proposed schedules, calendars, and financial information can be shared on-screen in real time.
  • Private breakout rooms. When the mediator needs to speak with each parent individually — or when either parent needs a moment to regroup — virtual breakout rooms allow private, one-on-one conversations without leaving the session.
