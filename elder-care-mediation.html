Elder Care and Guardianship Mediation

Few family conflicts carry as much emotional weight as disagreements about the care of an aging parent. Siblings who haven’t argued in decades find themselves in painful disputes about medical decisions, living arrangements, finances, and caregiving responsibilities. These conflicts aren’t really about logistics — they’re about love, fear, guilt, and family history.

Elder care mediation provides a compassionate, structured process for families to work through these disagreements together, with the guidance of a neutral mediator who understands both the emotional and practical dimensions of elder care decisions.

At Compass Mediation Group, Alisa Kharis brings a Juris Doctorate from Seattle University School of Law, 16 years of former legal practice in Washington state courts, 25+ years of mediation experience, and IMA-certified Master Mediator credentials to help families navigate these sensitive conversations. As an IRS Enrolled Agent and founder of Compass Tax Center, she can also address the financial and tax considerations that often arise when managing an elderly parent’s assets, estate, or care costs — including Medicaid implications, gifting rules, and trust management.

When Families Need Elder Care Mediation

👪

Disagreements About Living Arrangements

Should Mom stay in her home with in-home care? Move to assisted living? Move in with one of the children? These decisions involve medical realities, financial constraints, geographic limitations, and deeply personal preferences — both the parent’s and the adult children’s. When family members disagree, the conflict can become paralyzing, leaving everyone unhappy and the elderly parent caught in the middle.

⚖️

Guardianship and Conservatorship Disputes

When an aging parent can no longer make decisions independently, someone must step into that role. But who? Siblings may disagree about whether guardianship is even necessary, who should serve as guardian, and what powers the guardian should have. These disputes often end up in court, where a judge who doesn’t know the family makes the decision. Mediation allows the family to create a guardianship plan collaboratively — one that reflects the parent’s wishes and the family’s values.

📋

Medical Decision-Making Conflicts

End-of-life care, treatment options, medication management, and advance directive decisions are among the most emotionally charged issues families face. When siblings disagree about what Mom or Dad “would have wanted,” or when the parent’s own wishes conflict with what family members believe is best, mediation creates a safe space for these conversations to happen before they escalate into crises.

💰

Financial Management and Spending Disputes

Managing an elderly parent’s finances can expose long-standing family tensions. Who controls the accounts? Is the money being spent appropriately? Is one sibling taking advantage of their access? Are care expenses being shared fairly? These disputes frequently involve accusations and defensiveness. Mediation brings transparency and structure to financial discussions, helping families agree on budgets, spending oversight, and cost-sharing arrangements.

🤝

Caregiving Responsibility Imbalances

In most families, caregiving responsibilities fall disproportionately on one sibling — often the one who lives closest or who has the most flexible schedule. This imbalance breeds resentment, especially when other siblings are perceived as uninvolved or critical. Mediation helps families create fair caregiving plans that acknowledge each person’s capacity and contribution, whether that’s hands-on care, financial support, or coordination.

📦

Estate Planning Conflicts

When elder care decisions intersect with inheritance expectations, family dynamics get even more complicated. Should the family home be sold to pay for care? If one sibling provides unpaid caregiving, should they receive a larger share of the estate? These conversations are difficult, but having them proactively through mediation prevents much larger conflicts later.

👥

Sibling Disagreements About Care Quality

Sometimes the conflict isn’t about what type of care to provide — it’s about whether the current care is adequate. One sibling may feel the assisted living facility isn’t meeting Dad’s needs. Another may believe the in-home caregiver should be replaced. Mediation helps families evaluate care quality together and agree on standards, communication protocols, and decision-making processes.

Why Mediation Is Ideal for Elder Care Disputes

It Keeps Families Together

Elder care disputes have the power to permanently fracture family relationships. Litigation — especially guardianship litigation — is adversarial by nature. Family members become opposing parties, lawyers speak instead of siblings, and the damage to relationships often outlasts the parent’s lifetime. Mediation takes the opposite approach, treating family members as collaborators working toward a shared goal: the best possible care for their loved one.

It Centers the Elder’s Wishes

In court proceedings, the elderly parent’s voice is often lost in legal arguments. In mediation, the parent’s preferences, values, and expressed wishes are central to every decision. When the parent is able to participate, they’re included in the conversation. When they aren’t, the mediator helps family members focus on what their parent would want rather than what each sibling wants.

It’s Faster Than Court

Guardianship proceedings can take months, and the family conflict doesn’t pause while the case is pending. Mediation typically resolves elder care disputes in two to four sessions, allowing families to implement care plans quickly — which is especially important when health situations are deteriorating.

It’s Private

Family conflicts about money, medical decisions, and caregiving are deeply personal. Court proceedings create public records. Mediation keeps everything confidential, allowing family members to speak honestly without fear that their words will appear in court documents.

It’s More Flexible

Courts operate within rigid legal frameworks. Mediation allows families to create customized solutions — rotating caregiving schedules, shared financial oversight systems, trial living arrangements, regular family meetings, and other creative approaches that a judge simply can’t order.

How Elder Care Mediation Works at Compass Mediation Group

1

Step 1: Free Consultation

Reach out to us to discuss your family’s situation. We’ll listen, explain how mediation works, and help you determine whether it’s the right approach.

2

Step 2: Gathering Perspectives

Before the first session, we may speak individually with each family member to understand their concerns, fears, and priorities. This helps ensure everyone feels heard from the very beginning.

3

Step 3: Virtual Mediation Sessions

All sessions are conducted virtually, making it easy for family members in different cities or states to participate. Alisa guides the family through each issue — living arrangements, medical care, finances, caregiving responsibilities — in a structured but compassionate way. Her 25+ years of mediation experience and her background as a former practicing attorney in family law, civil litigation, and administrative hearings mean she can navigate both the emotional and legal dimensions of these conversations with confidence.

4

Step 4: Care Plan Agreement

The family’s decisions are documented in a written care plan agreement. This plan can cover immediate needs, future contingencies, communication protocols, and financial arrangements. It becomes the family’s roadmap, reducing future conflicts by providing clear answers to the questions that caused the original disagreements.

5

Step 5: Follow-Up Support

Elder care needs change over time. Compass Mediation Group offers follow-up sessions if the family needs to revisit or update their care plan as circumstances evolve.

Who Participates in Elder Care Mediation?

Elder care mediation can include:

  • Adult children and siblings — the most common participants
  • The elderly parent — when they are willing and able to participate
  • Spouses and partners of the adult children, when appropriate
  • Other family members — grandchildren, in-laws, or close family friends who play a role in care decisions
  • Professional advisors — financial planners, attorneys, geriatric care managers, or social workers can participate in relevant sessions

The mediator works with the family to determine who should be included to ensure the process is productive and fair.
